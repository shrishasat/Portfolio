2026

Written 2nd September 2026

The sequel that reads whole sentences

How much better can a mind-reading keyboard get in eighteen months? On 29th June 2026, Meta AI released Brain2Qwerty v2 alongside the peer-reviewed paper for v1 — timing that felt deliberate. V2 reasons over words and meaning instead of single characters, trained on around 22,000 sentences from nine volunteers wearing MEG helmets for hours at a time. Meta open-sourced the training code for both versions. How much of the remaining gap is engineering, and how much is something we haven't named yet?

Source: AI at Meta, Brain2Qwerty v2 announcement, 29 June 2026.

2025

Written 28th June 2025

Typing with your mind, one letter at a time

Can you type without touching a keyboard? In February 2025, Meta AI unveiled Brain2Qwerty — a system that reads EEG or MEG signals while someone silently types on a QWERTY layout, then a language model cleans up the guesses. MEG got the character error rate down near 19% for the best-trained participants; EEG lagged far behind. Still a lab rig, not a laptop. What happens the day the hardware finally catches up to the model?

Source: Meta AI, "Brain2Qwerty" announcement, Feb 2025.

2024

Written 3rd March 2024

Can a machine hear what you're only listening to?

What does it mean for an algorithm to eavesdrop on perception itself? Back in October 2023, Meta AI described a model that matched three seconds of MEG or EEG signal to the right spoken sentence out of a thousand candidates — up to 80% accuracy in their best participants, no implants involved. It's a contrastive model trained across 175 volunteers. If machines can decode what we hear, how long before they decode what we only intend?

Source: Défossez et al., "Decoding speech perception from non-invasive brain recordings," Nature Machine Intelligence, Oct 2023.

2019

Written 18th November 2019

Why does a continent burn before summer even starts?

In November 2019, bushfires tore through New South Wales and Queensland weeks ahead of the usual season, turning the sky over Sydney the color of rust. I kept coming back to one image: koalas moving faster than the smoke. It made me wonder how any brain — human or animal — decides, in half a second, whether to freeze or flee. I didn't have an answer then. I'm not sure I do now.
