2026
02 SEPTEMBER 2026

The sequel that reads whole sentences

How much better can a mind-reading keyboard get in eighteen months? On 29 June 2026, Meta AI released Brain2Qwerty v2 alongside the peer-reviewed paper for v1 — timing that felt deliberate. V2 reasons over words and meaning instead of single characters, trained on around 22,000 sentences from nine volunteers wearing MEG helmets for hours at a time.

Meta open-sourced the training code for both versions. How much of the remaining gap is engineering, and how much is something we haven't named yet?

Source: AI at Meta, Brain2Qwerty v2 announcement.
30 JUNE 2026

What's actually inside the hat that reads your keystrokes?

Everyone covered the headline number. Fewer people asked how Brain2Qwerty actually works. The pipeline is refreshingly unglamorous: a convolutional module compresses raw MEG or EEG traces into per-character embeddings, and a pretrained character-level language model then corrects the noisy guesses into plausible words.

The language model is doing more work than the brain signal in a lot of these cases — it's autocorrect with a magnetometer attached. If the decoder leans that heavily on the language prior, how much of "reading the brain" is actually reading the alphabet?

Source: Meta AI / FAIR, Brain2Qwerty peer-reviewed paper (released alongside v2).
2025
17 NOVEMBER 2025

How does the brain juggle sound, word and meaning at once?

Speech doesn't arrive one neat stage at a time — phonemes, words and meaning all have to be tracked simultaneously, without one overwriting another. Recording MEG from 21 people listening to two hours of stories, this study time-resolved how the brain maintains phonetic, word-form, syntactic and semantic features in parallel.

Each level of the hierarchy is held by its own dynamic neural code, moving at a speed matched to that level — slower, more abstract features drift more slowly across the brain. They call it hierarchical dynamic coding. If every level has its own clock, what happens when those clocks fall out of sync?

Source: Gwilliams, Marantz, Poeppel & King, PNAS.
23 AUGUST 2025

Can a computer imagine a sound it never heard you hear?

What did a bark, a violin, or a footstep sound like inside your head — literally, as brain activity? Researchers paired fMRI recordings of people listening to natural sounds with the hierarchical features of a deep neural network trained on audio, then fed decoded features into a generative model.

The reconstructions kept the timbre and short-term texture of speech, animal calls and instruments, even though longer temporal sequences fell apart. If short-term texture survives decoding but sequence doesn't, is rhythm stored somewhere else entirely?

Source: Park, Tsukamoto, Tanaka & Kamitani, PLOS Biology.
24 JULY 2025

A wristband that reads intention before the muscle moves

What if the interface for every device you own was just... your wrist? Meta's Reality Labs introduced a generic, non-invasive neuromotor interface built on surface EMG worn at the wrist, decoding the motor-unit action potentials that precede visible movement into control signals for human-computer interaction.

No calibration per person, no implant, and it generalised across a wide population of users. It's a strange middle ground between a mouse and a mind-reader. Where exactly does "reading movement" stop and "reading intention" begin?

Source: Kaifosh et al., Meta Reality Labs, Nature.
12 JULY 2025

Can two strangers' brains be translated into each other?

If no two brains are wired identically, can a model trained on one person's neural code say anything about another's? This paper built a neural code converter that translates brain activity patterns across individuals and across recording sites, without requiring the two people to have seen the same stimuli during training.

That's the part that stopped me — no shared stimuli, and it still converts. It suggests something about neural representations is more universal than the idiosyncratic wiring of any one brain. How far can that generalise — across species, even?

Source: Wang et al., Nature.
10 JULY 2025

What does outsourcing your essay do to your brain?

Fifty-four students wrote SAT-style essays using either ChatGPT, a search engine, or just their own heads, while wearing EEG. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest neural connectivity, struggled to quote their own essays back, and reported the least sense of ownership over what they'd written — even after four months of practice.

The researchers call it "cognitive debt" — effort deferred, not eliminated. When a brain-only writer later switched to the LLM, their engagement dropped fast. What does it cost to get that engagement back once it's gone?

Source: Kosmyna et al., MIT Media Lab (preprint).
04 JULY 2025

What can a language model know without a body?

Can you understand "sour" if you've never tasted anything? This study compared how large language models and humans represent thousands of everyday concepts across non-sensorimotor, sensory and motor dimensions — the kind of meaning norms collected by asking real people to rate words.

LLMs tracked human representations well for abstract, non-sensorimotor concepts, but the match got weaker moving into sensory features and nearly vanished for motor ones. Language alone gets you most of the way to meaning — just not all the way. What's missing in that last stretch?

Source: Xu, Peng, Nastase, Chodorow, Wu & Li, Nature Human Behaviour.
28 JUNE 2025

Typing with your mind, one letter at a time

Can you type without touching a keyboard? In February 2025, Meta AI unveiled Brain2Qwerty — a system that reads EEG or MEG signals while someone silently types on a QWERTY layout, then a language model cleans up the guesses.

MEG achieved substantially better character decoding than EEG in their experiments. Still a laboratory system rather than a laptop. What happens when the hardware finally catches up to the model?

Source: Meta AI, Brain2Qwerty.
15 JUNE 2025

Before neuroscience, there was telepathy

Long before MEG helmets and decoders, people were already asking whether one mind could reach another without a channel in between. Two old texts from my reading pile: Stephan Braude's writing on telepathic cognition from 1978, and Vladimir Bekhterev's much earlier work on the phenomena of human psychic activity from 1959.

Neither holds up as neuroscience by today's standards, but the questions they were circling — shared representation, distant minds, unexplained correlation — are the same ones Brain2Qwerty and neural code conversion are still chasing. Are we just answering an old question with better tools?

Source: Braude (1978); Bekhterev (1959).
21 MAY 2025

Reconstructing an image from a brain that won't sit still

Most brain-to-image decoders quietly throw away time — they collapse an fMRI response into a single averaged snapshot before reconstructing anything. Meta's Dynadiff skips that step entirely, training a single-stage diffusion model directly on the continuously evolving fMRI signal.

It beat prior multi-stage pipelines on time-resolved data while staying competitive on the old averaged benchmarks, and it lets researchers watch an image representation sharpen moment to moment inside the brain. What does the half-formed image look like, right before it resolves?

Source: Careil, Benchetrit & King, FAIR at Meta.
20 FEBRUARY 2025

Enough finger control to fly a drone with your thoughts

Can a paralysed person get the same idle-hours payoff the rest of us get from a video game? This intracortical brain-computer interface decoded four independent degrees of finger movement — three finger groups plus a two-dimensional thumb — from one participant with paralysis.

The team then mapped that decoded finger control onto a virtual quadcopter, and the participant flew it through obstacle courses for fun, not therapy. Leisure, not just function, might be the more honest benchmark for a BCI. Why did it take this long for someone to measure that?

Source: Willsey et al., Nature Medicine.
17 FEBRUARY 2025

Teaching a bionic hand to feel an edge

A single point of electrical stimulation in somatosensory cortex can make a paralysed person feel a tingle — but an edge, or something moving across the skin, is a shape, not a point. This team patterned intracortical microstimulation across multiple electrodes to encode arbitrary tactile shapes and skin-indentation patterns.

By timing the stimulation across electrodes, they could evoke a sensation of motion whose speed and direction the person could actually distinguish. Touch, it turns out, is encoded in the choreography between electrodes, not any one of them. How much finer can that choreography get?

Source: Valle et al., Science.
07 FEBRUARY 2025

Watching a thought turn into a sentence

Somewhere between having an idea and saying it out loud, the brain converts a thought into a specific sequence of words — and nobody really knows where that happens. Meta AI, working with the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language, built a model tracking exactly this transformation using non-invasive brain recordings.

It's a companion piece to the typing decoder — less about restoring communication, more about locating the moment language production actually starts inside the head. If we can watch the moment thought becomes syntax, what does that say about where a "thought" ends and "language" begins?

Source: AI at Meta, in collaboration with BCBL.
06 JANUARY 2025

One electrode isn't a fingertip. Twenty-five might be.

Real touch recruits dozens of nerve fibres at once — so why were researchers restoring touch through bionic hands one electrode at a time? This study tracked participants for two to eight years and found that stimulating multiple electrodes simultaneously sharply improved both tactile localisation and intensity discrimination over single-electrode stimulation.

Natural touch typically activates 25 or more electrodes at once; this work is still catching up to that number. If the gap to natural touch is mostly a matter of scaling up electrode count, what's actually stopping us from just doing that?

Source: Greenspon et al., Nature Biomedical Engineering.

24 SEPTEMBER 2024

What space quietly does to a brain

Astronauts come back taller, disoriented, and — it turns out — structurally different in the head. A run of studies now tracks this: ventricular volume increases with cumulative time in space and is accelerated on long-duration flights (Roberts et al.), fluid shifts and tissue-volume changes appear after 6–12 months aboard station (Van Ombergen et al.; Hupfeld et al., Cerebral Cortex Communications), and combined spaceflight stressors have been linked to depression-like and cognitive effects via neuroplasticity (Yin et al.). A related review catalogues the sensory disruptions astronauts face in flight and possible countermeasures (White et al.).

None of this is a single injury — it's cumulative drift, a brain slowly renegotiating its own fluid mechanics with nowhere to settle. If gravity has been quietly shaping human brain structure this whole time, what else has it been shaping that we've never had a way to test?

Source: Roberts et al. (2021); Van Ombergen et al. (2018); Yin et al. (2023); Hupfeld et al., Cerebral Cortex Communications (2020); White et al. (2025).
03 MARCH 2024

Can a machine hear what you're only listening to?

What does it mean for an algorithm to eavesdrop on perception itself? In 2023, Meta AI described a model that matched short segments of MEG or EEG activity to spoken sentences.

No implants. No conscious button press. Just neural activity and a model trying to infer which linguistic representation was being experienced.

Source: Défossez et al., Nature Machine Intelligence.
25 JANUARY 2024

Sending a robot dog to fetch water with a glance

Could a wearable, non-invasive BCI actually be useful outside a lab, today? Researchers fitted a pair of glasses with EEG and EOG electrodes and used them to control Boston Dynamics' Spot, letting a user answer simple yes/no questions through a mental-arithmetic task, each answer triggering a pre-set sequence of actions for the robot.

Spot walked across a room, picked up a bottle of water, and brought it back — an 83.4% success rate, aimed squarely at people with locked-in syndrome or ALS. It's an early prototype, not a product. What's the smallest vocabulary of "yes" and "no" a person actually needs to live more independently?

Source: Kosmyna, Hauptmann & Hmaidan, Sensors.
2023
03 MARCH 2023

Does your brain predict a sentence before it hears the end of it?

Language models predict the next word. Does the brain do something similar, or something stranger? Meta AI researchers analysed fMRI recordings of 304 people listening to short stories and found that language-model activations do map onto brain responses to speech — but only partially.

Enhancing the models with predictions spanning multiple timescales improved the match, and those longer-range predictions were concentrated in frontoparietal cortex, while temporal cortex stuck to shorter-range ones. A hierarchy of predictions, not just one. If the brain is predicting several timescales in parallel, which one is actually "understanding"?

Source: Caucheteux, Gramfort & King, Meta AI, Nature Human Behaviour.

The more we learn, the less we realise we know

A page from my personal notebook, not a paper — the kind of line that shows up uninvited at the end of a long day of reading and doesn't leave. It feels less like a conclusion and more like a description of what it's actually like to be alive and paying attention.

Maybe the world isn't something to be understood at all — maybe it's only meant to be experienced. Every entry on this page is an attempt to understand something anyway. Is that a contradiction, or just what curiosity actually looks like from the inside?

Why does a continent burn before summer even starts?

In November 2019, bushfires tore through New South Wales and Queensland weeks ahead of the usual season, turning the sky over Sydney the colour of rust.

I kept coming back to one image: koalas moving faster than the smoke. It made me wonder how any brain — human or animal — decides, in half a second, whether to freeze or flee.

05 OCTOBER 2018

Is looking at something the same as imagining it?

Motor imagery dominates BCI research — imagine moving a hand, decode that. This study asked a quieter question instead: can EEG tell apart actually observing an image from merely picturing it afterwards? Twenty-six people watched a flower or a hammer, then imagined the same cue from memory.

Visual imagery versus observation classified at 71% accuracy, and alpha-power changes carried most of that signal. A quieter, less-explored control channel than motor imagery, hiding in plain sight the whole time. Why did it take this long for visual imagery to get its own BCI paradigm?

Source: Kosmyna, Lindgren & Lécuyer, Scientific Reports.
